ALICE 101

New to ALICE? Start here.

Five minutes on this page covers what ALICE means, the key terms and the questions people ask most. All definitions and figures come from United For ALICE and the 2026 State of ALICE in Iowa report.

What is ALICE?

ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. ALICE households earn more than the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) but not enough to afford the basic cost of living in the county where they live. Many earn too much to qualify for public assistance.

Lead with people, support with data. ALICE Message Framework, Iowa ALICE Summit, June 2026

Key Terms

TermPlain-language meaning
ALICE householdIncome above the FPL but below what it costs to afford the basics in their county.
Household Survival BudgetThe bare-minimum cost of housing, child care, food, transportation, health care and technology, plus taxes. It leaves no room for emergencies, debt or savings.
ALICE ThresholdThe minimum average income a household needs to afford the basics, based on the Survival Budget and calculated for every U.S. county.
Below the ALICE ThresholdHouseholds in poverty plus ALICE households, combined.
ALICE Essentials IndexTracks how fast the cost of essentials rises over time. The CPI includes non-essentials as well.
ALICE Household Stability BudgetA higher budget for staying financially stable over time. It adds savings for emergencies, education or buying a home.

Why the Federal Poverty Level Falls Short

The FPL formula dates from the 1960s and uses one standard for the whole country. In Iowa in 2024:

HouseholdFederal Poverty LevelALICE Household Survival Budget (Iowa)
Single adult$15,060$27,732
Family of four (two adults, an infant, a preschooler)$31,200$74,616

The 2026 report also notes that the ALICE Essentials Index rose faster in Iowa than the national CPI: 4.1% a year from 2021 to 2024, compared with 2.8% a year from 2007 to 2019.

ALICE in Iowa: Key Findings (2024 Data)

  • Of Iowa's 1,329,232 households, 11% were in poverty and another 23% were ALICE, so 34% were below the ALICE Threshold.
  • Iowa's rate is lower than the national average (41%). Iowa ranks 4th lowest among the 50 states and D.C.
  • Hardship varied by county, from 47% below the Threshold in Decatur County to 26% in Dallas County.
  • Groups with the highest rates below the Threshold included Black households (58%), American Indian/Alaska Native households (50%), households headed by people under 25 (56%) or 65 and over (44%), and single-parent households (67% single-female-headed, 41% single-male-headed).
  • Accommodation and Food Services had the highest hardship of the 10 largest industries: 43% of its workers lived below the Threshold.
  • 22% of workers in Iowa's 20 most common occupations lived below the Threshold.
  • Between 2010 and 2024, the share of households below the Threshold ranged from 33% to 37%.

Source: United For ALICE, State of ALICE in Iowa 2026 report (2024 data).

Common Questions

How current is the data?

The 2026 report uses 2024 data, built from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey and public cost data. Iowa United Ways say this two-year lag is the biggest limit for timely communications. Pair ALICE figures with more current local data, such as free and reduced-price lunch eligibility, food pantry use or 211 trends.

Does every ALICE household have someone working?

No. ALICE measures cover all household types, including households headed by people 65 and over, 44% of whom were below the Threshold in 2024. Expect questions about the "E" for Employed. For exact definitions, use the Methodology.

What doesn't the Survival Budget include?

It is a bare-minimum budget with no room for emergencies, debt or savings. Debt was raised as a gap at the June 2026 Iowa ALICE Summit. The Stability Budget is the version that includes savings.

Someone says the ALICE data isn't valid. What do I say?

Point to the Methodology and the Research Advisory Committees, and note that the data comes from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. United For ALICE's FAQs (May 2025) and Research Advisory Committee Overview are in the Partner Network Folder's Basic Materials. Iowa United Ways that face pushback recommend backing ALICE with up to three other local data points and a real story, rather than leaning on one statistic.

GapStatewide talking points on data validity have been requested but not written yet. They are on the Roadmap.
Are college students counted?

This question comes up in university communities. The source materials reviewed for this hub don't answer it. Check the Methodology or ask United For ALICE through their contact page.

Can we use ALICE to measure our own outcomes?

Carefully. One lesson from the listening conversations: ALICE is strong for framing and advocacy but weak for measuring program outcomes. The data is two years old, isn't tracked household by household, and moves with many outside factors. One Iowa United Way dropped an ALICE-based community goal for these reasons. Consider pairing ALICE framing with program-level metrics.

Tips From Iowa United Ways

  • Mind the lag. The current release reflects 2024. Say so, and add a more current local data point.
  • Keep it to three. Choose up to three data points and pair them with a story, rather than one big ALICE claim.
  • Pair ALICE with labor-market data. One Iowa United Way matched the ALICE living-wage benchmark with its Chamber's priority-jobs list. That showed which in-demand jobs pay a living wage, and it worked with community colleges to line up training programs.
  • Use the From Data to Action section of the 2026 report. It shows how meeting basic needs in housing, child care, food and transportation benefits the wider community, and peers say it is underused.

Core Message: The Poverty Line Is Outdated

Local United Ways have asked for help explaining this, especially to legislators.

  • The FPL formula dates from the 1960s and applies one standard nationwide.
  • In Iowa (2024), a family of four needed $74,616 to cover the basics. The FPL for that family was $31,200.
  • The CPI tracks 200+ goods and services, including non-essentials. The ALICE Essentials Index tracks only the basics.
  • Source: 2026 State of ALICE in Iowa report.

Words That Land

What Iowa United Ways have found works. Wording varies by audience, so test it locally.

Instead of…Try…Why
"Working poor""People who are working but struggling" · "Families who struggle to consistently make a basic-needs budget"Less stigmatizing. Works with conservative audiences.
"Poverty" (with business audiences)"Working families" · "Affordability crisis" · "Earning enough to support your family" · "Low to moderate income""Poverty" can feel accusatory to business leaders.
Opening with the dollar thresholdOpening with a family's storyIn some rooms, many people are ALICE themselves.
Leading with the acronymLeading with the idea, then naming ALICEALICE usually needs explaining, and audiences vary in how much.

United Ways don't agree on everything. Some use "making ends meet" publicly, and others avoid it. Some use the ALICE brand heavily, and others mostly use ALICE data internally and frame it in local terms. Both are valid. Choose what fits your community.

Sample Mailer Language

A volunteer-run Iowa United Way used this in its annual donor mailer. Swap in your county's numbers from the Mapping Tool:

"In [County], [X]% of households are ALICE (asset limited, income constrained, employed). These households earn more than the federal poverty level yet not enough to afford their basic needs. A single adult in [County] needs $[X] a year, and a family with young children needs $[X], just to get by. Wages aren't keeping pace with rising costs, and that's why your partnership matters."

When You Hear "They Just Need to Work Harder"

Walk through the chain of obstacles instead of arguing. The car breaks down, so there's no way to get to work. There's no money to fix the car without that work. Rent within walking distance of jobs costs more than an entry-level wage covers. Then show the Cost of Basics next to local wages.

By Audience

Legislators and local officials
  • Tie numbers to local stories, and use trusted local relationships to open doors.
  • Bring district-level numbers from the Legislative District Tool.
  • Iowa United Ways have used ALICE percentages in conversations about education and child care.
  • Use the ALICE budget as a policy map: link each cost barrier to a possible employer incentive or policy response.
  • For county officials, break ALICE down by town and show how many people you serve in each. One Iowa United Way won over its board of supervisors this way.
Employers and business leaders
  • Frame it as good for business: retention, recruitment and productivity, not charity.
  • Show the ALICE Wage Tool and the Cost of Basics.
  • Model to explore: United Way of Greater Chattanooga's "United for Working Families" approach, which offers family-friendly workplace strategies such as child care, transportation stipends and parental leave. Full notes on ALICE in the Workplace. Its ALICE at Work webinar frames it simply: "you don't have to be in poverty to be struggling," from bank tellers to CNAs to phlebotomists.
  • Also worth a look: Trident United Way's Thrive at Work, which embeds family coaches with employers. Companies can direct up to 10% of their campaign to a fund for their own employees.
  • Start with the CEO, not HR, and ask for one change at a time. Many companies already offer benefits employees don't use, and helping them promote those benefits is an easy first win.
  • Be sensitive: in some workplaces, many employees in the room are ALICE.
Nonprofit partners and grant reviewers
  • Some United Ways cover ALICE in grant-reviewer training and grant-writing workshops.
  • Some ask applicants what share of the people they serve are ALICE.
  • Hard question worth raising: do nonprofits themselves pay ALICE wages?
Community and media
  • Stories first. Invite people to share theirs at ALICE Voices.
  • Use no more than three data points, from different sources, so no single source carries the claim.
  • In small towns, use a composite family instead of a real person, so no one is identified.
  • Media resources: United For ALICE Press Center.

Experience Tough Choices: An ALICE Life Journey

An ALICE life simulation for United Way events, grounded in Iowa ALICE data.

Start the simulation

About the Simulation

Tough Choices puts participants in the shoes of an ALICE household. According to the build notes:

  • Participants join on their own phones with a session PIN, so it works for a room of any size.
  • Personas are based on real ALICE profiles.
  • Each month brings a paycheck, bills and a major event. Real choices have consequences that build up, and the simulation tracks stress, health, job security and relationships.
  • The end-of-simulation summary shows the cost of poverty, such as late fees and missed wages.
  • Facilitators can add events mid-session, such as a rent increase or a layoff. Discussion questions are provided at the close.
  • Cost data can be set to Iowa statewide or to a local region.

It was built because peers found that existing ALICE activities lack real choices and don't show how costs compound. A phone-based format with real choices was specifically requested. Peers have also asked for a version for high school students, since some schools already run a "Game of Life" budgeting event.

Running a Session

Click items to check them off as you prepare.

Before

  • Open the link on the device you'll present from and test it.
  • Check Wi-Fi and projector, and make sure participants have phones.
  • Decide whether to use statewide or local cost data.

During

  • Brief participants: one household, real choices, no right answers.
  • Consider adding a mid-session event that fits your community, such as a rent increase.

Debrief prompts

  • Which choice was hardest, and why?
  • What surprised you about how costs added up?
  • What one thing would have changed your household's path?
  • Where do you see ALICE in our community, and what can we do about it together?

After

  • Submit Simulation feedback through the Feedback form, with your audience and number of participants.

National Models: ALICE in the Workplace

How two United Ways turned ALICE into employer partnerships.

Notes from two United Way Worldwide Innovations at Every Scale webinars (Oct 2026): Trident United Way's Thrive at Work and United Way of Greater Chattanooga's United for Working Families. Recordings and slides are in UW Learn. Figures are as the presenters reported them, not independently verified. Contact each United Way to learn more.
Why it mattersAt a glanceChattanoogaTridentQuestions to consider

Why These Matter for Iowa

  • The "E" in ALICE is employed. Both United Ways go to where ALICE already is: the workplace.
  • Both turned ALICE from a message into a corporate partnership, and both say corporate giving grew as a result, including new campaigns from companies that never ran one.
  • Iowa United Ways have already asked about these models. See Peer Practices and the Messaging Toolkit.

United Way of Greater Chattanooga: United for Working Families

Where it came from

  • Tennessee ranked 49th in the nation for helping children and families escape poverty. The United Way adopted ALICE in 2018, then spent six months in 2019 following 36 single working ALICE moms.
  • What families need came down to three places: home, community and work. That led to a two-generation, whole-family strategy.
  • 44% of children in Hamilton County grow up in ALICE households. In their words, you can't "social service" your way to uplifting nearly half a community.
  • Cost of living rose about $23,000 for a family in three years, and 70% of Tennessee's most common jobs pay under $20 an hour.

How they frame it for employers

  1. Wages matter.
  2. Family-stabilizing benefits matter more: child care, transportation, health and well-being.
  3. Workforce development matters, because some jobs were never designed to support a family.
  4. Community supports matter, and the point is to bring them, including 211, into the workplace.

Their five pillars of a family-friendly workplace: flexibility, wellness, economic stability, child care and family assistance, and social connectedness ("the secret sauce").

How it grew

  • It started as a one-time event to replace a campaign kickoff that would have cost over $100,000. The response was strong enough that it became a program with a full-time staff person.
  • Annual summit that opens with an executive-only breakfast. HR leaders like it because their CEO comes back asking about child care.
  • Family Friendly Workplace Guide for executives and HR, full of local company case studies. Companies like seeing themselves in it.
  • 11 industry convenings each summer (health care, finance, insurance, government and others) replaced one-on-one CEO visits. Peers bring peers.
  • ALICE at Work: simulations using Mentimeter, developed with United Way of Northern New Jersey, now replace the standard campaign presentation.
  • United at Work: deploying resources inside companies, similar to Trident's model.

Results they reported

  • 60+ employers engaged, some with no prior United Way relationship, reaching 100,000+ employees and an estimated 40,000 ALICE families.
  • 31,000 local workers reached through ALICE at Work cohorts over two years.
  • The event went from costing $50,000 to $75,000 to earning $65,000+ in sponsorships in year one and six figures in year two. Their top sponsor ($80,000) renewed.
  • Employer changes: Coca-Cola raised frontline wages after the simulations. Blue Cross Blue Shield is overhauling frontline benefits. Hamilton County government added parental leave. The county school system added financial and housing training for teachers. Others added on-site child care, shift swapping, on-site counseling and leadership training.
  • In their latest donor survey, "working families" was the most understood and most compelling message at every giving level.

Reframing the campaign goal

Dollar goals felt "stale," and volunteers didn't connect with them. The new goal ties to 10,320 ALICE households with children in the county: 10,320 donors and 10,320 employees reached through workplace programs. Campaign chairs and corporate leaders embraced it right away.

Lessons they shared

  • Put the work with whoever holds corporate relationships, usually the resource development team, not program staff.
  • Start with CEOs. HR enthusiasm stalls without the CEO.
  • Dispel the "finger-wagging" fear early. Position it as partnership, not prescription.
  • Ask for one pillar at a time, not all five.
  • Quick win: many companies already offer good benefits that employees don't use. A family benefits fair, where spouses and kids came too, raised use at one company.
  • Build fluency first. If your staff and board can't explain ALICE, start there.
  • Partner with local experts, such as early childhood organizations, instead of claiming expertise.
  • Be authentic. This works because the goal is better impact for ALICE, not just a new way to raise money.
  • Local matters. What works in Chattanooga won't translate directly to a rural Iowa county.
  • They applied ALICE at Work to themselves, changing their parental leave, their wage structure for hourly staff and their 403(b).

Family-Friendly Workplace Guide

Family-Friendly Workplace Guide (United Way of Greater Chattanooga, sponsored by Tennessee Valley Authority; developed with Family Forward NC; ©2026 North Carolina Early Childhood Foundation). 73 pages.

The employer-facing guide Chattanooga hands to executives and HR teams. It is a model for what an Iowa version could look like; its laws, wages and resources are specific to Tennessee.

  • Section 1, the case: "Business-smart, family-friendly, future-ready." Opens with Hamilton County's ALICE numbers (more than 1 in 3 households; 10,320 households with children), the five pillars and an ALICE Survival vs. Stability budget table by household type.
  • Section 2, policies: each policy gets a one-page "benefits at a glance" for employers, children and families, the research behind it, national practice and a QR code to sample policies.
    • Paid leave and wages: parental, sick and safe, family and medical, parental involvement leave, family-sustaining wage
    • Flexible work: flextime, working from home, job sharing or part-time, predictable scheduling
    • Health and wellness: insurance and wellness, EAPs, flexible spending accounts
    • Child care: on-site or consortium, subsidized or referral, backup care
    • Accommodations: breastfeeding support, pregnancy accommodations, babies at work
    • Local case studies: Cempa Community Care and Unum Group
  • Section 3, getting started: steps for employers (know the law, assess benefits, survey employees, count the cost of doing nothing, help employees use what you already offer, build the culture), ways business leaders can advocate, and steps for employees.
  • Ready-to-use tools: a sample employee benefits survey, a child care needs assessment and a benefits worksheet.

Ideas to borrow for Iowa: pair each policy with an Iowa ALICE budget figure, swap in Iowa laws and resources, and feature Iowa employers as case studies.

Trident United Way: Thrive at Work

Where it came from

  • An 82-year-old United Way on its fifth business model. Revenue fell from $14 million to $5 million and has nearly doubled since.
  • It cut 28 programs to 4 and rehomed the others with community nonprofits, to focus on one mission.
  • 38% of the region's 130,000+ households are ALICE. About 35,000 households with a child are below the ALICE Threshold. A family of four with a child in care needs over $91,000 a year in Charleston.
  • Bold goal: uplift 15,000 working families with a child out of poverty by 2035, where "out of poverty" means moving forward on the ALICE Threshold, not the Federal Poverty Level. The board halved the 35,000 figure to keep the goal achievable.

The model

A family coach works with a family for 12 to 24 months to raise income, improve credit and debt-to-income, build a budget and savings, and claim benefits left on the table. The same coaching model is offered in different settings:

  • Thrive at Work: coaches embedded with an employer's HR team.
  • Thrive at School: helps principals and counselors keep families from being evicted mid-year (98% or more prevented, they report).
  • Thrive at Home: on-site at affordable housing, paid for by the owners (700+ units).
  • Thrive at Worship, Thrive at Centers (four centers) and Thrive at Military.

How Thrive at Work works

  • The employer pays a program management fee from its HR budget and funds a financial stability pool for its own employees.
  • In a crisis, such as a $90 flat tire, the coach pays the vendor directly, never the employee, then keeps working with the employee.
  • Confidential: employers get only quarterly totals by need (housing, food and so on), never names.
  • The employer sets the per-person cap and which services are included. Marketing never advertises the cap, so people reach out only when they need help.
  • Companies can direct up to 10% of their campaign to their own pool.
  • Each launch is a branded, upbeat company kickoff, so using the service doesn't feel embarrassing. QR codes go straight to a coach.
  • Add-ons: financial classes taught by a bank partner, VITA tax help, on-site resource fairs.

One coach serves about 100 to 150 clients a year.

Results they reported

  • First pilot, with a construction company: retention rose from about 70% to 92% for employees who used coaching.
  • 8 employer partners, about 9 proposals pending and about 10 more companies expected in January.
  • Corporate campaigns up 18% last year, 7 new corporate campaigns this year, individual giving up 35%, and Tocqueville donors from 42 to 97.
  • The state's largest employer (about 38,000 employees) re-engaged after six years away, and asked to restart its campaign without being asked.
  • A $12 million, five-year growth campaign raised $4.5 million in year one against a $4 million goal. Family coaches went from 0 to 13 in a year, with 30 planned.

211 and referrals

  • 211 now doubles as a 24/7 intake line with a warm handoff to a family coach, so families tell their story once.
  • 24 nonprofits have signed agreements for full closed-loop referrals with follow-up.

Lessons they shared

  • Focus. One clear goal resonated with funders and kept staff on what matters most.
  • Join fundraising and mission. The advancement team sells Thrive at Work, writes the agreement and handles stewardship; program staff deliver it.
  • Talk to major donors and companies before you change. They lost only one $25,000 donor, who returned a year later.
  • Change takes people. They had about 80% staff turnover over three years to build the right team.
  • Nonprofit partners found the shift rocky at first, then found their place in the work.

Status and contact

  • In a "grow and verify" phase, with a third-party evaluator checking results. They have trademarked the Thrive components and hope to offer them across the network.
  • United Ways can email Trident to join a waitlist for future pilots, expected around February to June.
  • Website: tuw.org/thrive-at

Questions to Consider Locally

  • Which corporate partner, ideally a manufacturer with many ALICE employees, would pilot a workplace approach with you?
  • Could part of your kickoff or event budget fund an employer summit instead?
  • Could your campaign goal be stated as a number of ALICE households, donors or employees, not dollars?
  • Can your own staff and board explain ALICE today? Both United Ways said to start there.
  • What would ALICE at Work show about your own United Way's wages and benefits?

Peer Practices: What Iowa United Ways Are Doing

What's working in other Iowa communities.

Highlights from listening conversations with Local United Ways, Sept–Nov 2026. These are summaries, not official positions. Contact the United Way directly to learn more.

Themes So Far

  • ALICE works best as a framing and decision-making tool. Many United Ways use ALICE data internally and frame it publicly in local terms. Outcomes are better measured at the program level.
  • Stories beat statistics. A composite local "ALICE family" story, tied to your own programs, outperforms data-heavy decks. Composites also protect privacy in small towns.
  • Messaging should fit the audience. Business, nonprofit, legislative and community audiences each need different framing, and dollar figures can land badly in rooms where many people are ALICE.
  • Community-level data opens doors. Breaking ALICE down by town or legislative district, and tying it to the people you serve, persuades local officials.
  • United Ways are at very different stages. Some have used ALICE for 10 years; some volunteer-run boards learned about it recently. Resources need to come in sizes, from a first fact sheet to full campaign kits.
  • People want one easy shared place, not SharePoint folders or spreadsheets. That request is why this hub exists.
  • The two-year data lag is the most common frustration.

By United Way

United Way of Dubuque

  • Built its annual campaign kickoff around ALICE for about 175 attendees: "Hi, my name is Alice" name tags, a board-game simulation, a local video and a board-chair activity. Attendees were asked for a non-financial pledge, such as telling five people.
  • Produced an 11-minute local ALICE video featuring three community members who had been ALICE, then cut clips for social media.
  • Followed up one-on-one with leaders who missed the kickoff, including the mayor.
  • Covers ALICE in grant-reviewer training and community grant-writing sessions. It explored asking applicants what share of the people they serve are ALICE, but kept the poverty measure this cycle while partners learn more.
  • Reports local financial institutions, city government and a community plan using ALICE data.

United Way of the Quad Cities

  • Uses ALICE to set and track a community living-wage goal: the share of young adults earning a living wage rose from 63% to 71% over about five years.
  • Paired ALICE data with the Chamber's priority-jobs report to launch Pathways to Living Wage Careers with community colleges, using a $24-an-hour living wage for one parent and one child.
  • Treats ALICE mainly as an internal decision tool and uses "affordability crisis" in public.
  • Proposed a coordinated statewide media push at each ALICE data release, with every United Way localizing the story and tracking coverage, and making ALICE a through-line in the state legislative agenda.

United Way of Muscatine

  • More than 10 years of ALICE use: county one-pagers for CEO visits, a grant writers' collaborative with shared data links, and ALICE percentages in conversations with legislators about education and child care.
  • Tells a composite local ALICE family story and matches each barrier the family faces to a program the United Way runs.
  • Frames ALICE for conservative audiences as "earning enough to support their family," and adjusts whether it uses the acronym by audience.
  • Lesson shared: keep ALICE as a framing tool and anchor outcomes to program-level metrics. It retired an ALICE-based community goal because the data lags and outside events swamp local progress.

United Way of Story County

  • Pairs ALICE data with other local measures, such as free and reduced-price lunch and food pantry data, and impact stories. Uses no more than three data points.
  • Keeps the ALICE brand in the background publicly and uses "low to moderate income." It has fielded public questions about ALICE data, especially the "Employed" in ALICE and whether college students are counted.
  • Finds that "working families" framing is gaining traction as costs rise.

Waverly-Shell Rock Area United Way

  • With a 9% poverty rate, uses ALICE alongside poverty as the core of its case for support.
  • Ran an ALICE skit for one campaign: "Alice" is handed a backpack, a doll, a dollhouse and diapers, and United Way programs lift each one off.
  • Broke ALICE down by community and tied it to service counts for the county board of supervisors, who each sent a thank-you.
  • Presents ALICE yearly at quarterly meetings for funded and unfunded partners, and teaches partners to pull data for other counties.
  • Learned that 45% of its local ALICE population is seniors, and opened or added services for older adults as a result.
  • Says up front that ALICE can't confirm employment, and refers to "Alice" as a person without spelling out the acronym.

United Way of Johnson & Washington Counties

  • Sees ALICE as a statewide opportunity with employers and in advocacy.
  • Recommends United Way of Chattanooga's ALICE at Work story: "you don't have to be in poverty to be struggling," from bank tellers to CNAs.
  • Interested in Michigan's Tri-Share child care model as a solution built for ALICE families.

United Way of Winneshiek County

  • An all-volunteer board that began using ALICE about two years ago.
  • Added ALICE to its annual donor mailer: "In Winneshiek County, 34% of households are ALICE (asset limited, income constrained, employed). These households earn more than the federal poverty level yet not enough to afford their basic needs." It closed with "that's why your partnership matters."
  • Asked for a simple ALICE fact sheet, quick event ideas, flowcharts and postcard-size graphics for small and volunteer-run United Ways.

United Way of East Central Iowa

  • Built an internal Notion dashboard that brings program data and county ALICE data together for meeting prep. It is the model for this hub, and an ALICE dashboard prototype was shared with the United Ways of Iowa board.
  • Built the Tough Choices ALICE simulation.

Mason City (Feedback)

  • Local United Ways want help explaining to legislators why the Federal Poverty Level is outdated.
